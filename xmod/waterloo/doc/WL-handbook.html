1. Introduction 
1.1: An Overview of the Project 
A description of the Waterloo Directories project and its history is available in the introductory notes to Phase 1 as well as on the advertising brochure for Phase 2. However, to give you an idea of where you fit into the project, here is a brief description of the steps involved in gathering and processing information.

The process begins with a list of English titles gathered from various secondary sources: Phase 1 of the Waterloo Directory, library catalogues, union lists and so on. The list is given to researchers who go to the major libraries in England and look at the primary sources. There, researchers record as much information (according to the various fields) as possible on the titles and collect photo facsimiles of the title pages of each publication.

The information is then relayed to the University of Waterloo where editors ‘clean up’ the data on microcomputers according to predetermined rules of style. This is also the stage at which duplicate entries of the same periodicals are merged.

When the information has passed through this stage, it is sent to CMS (the UW mainframe computer) using a program called MSKermit. In CMS it is converted by means of an xedit macro into a form acceptable to SPIRES. SPIRES is a system for storing and sorting large numbers of records in one database. In it, the records are sorted alphabetically and numbered, and subject, person, and place indexes are generated along with more cross references. The records in SPIRES are then converted into yet another form using something called SCRIPT. This format allows them to be typeset by computer. Once laser printouts are available, the text and photo facsimile title pages are mounted and the work is sent off to the printer.

1.3.1: A Periodical Definition 

The broadest definition of a periodical is ‘any publication which appears regularly (up to annually) for which no ending date is fixed at the date of the publication’s inception’.

However, bibliographers do not always agree on which types of publications should be included as periodicals. The researcher should be familiar with our definition of a periodical and know which publications are relevant to phase two of this project. British librarians and scholars often use the term ‘serials’ as synonymous with ‘periodicals’. For us, a ‘serial’ would include such things as a book published in four parts—which is NOT included in our definition of a periodical.

1.3.4: Definition of an 'English' Periodical 
 The Directory of English Newspapers and Magazines, 1800-1900 lists periodicals published in 19thcentury England. The directory includes those periodicals:
  1. - where an English town or city is a place of publication of the periodical

    NOTES: - can be published simultaneously in another city or country

    - the periodical may have been published in England for only a short period of its life

    - the islands of Guernsey, Jersey & Isle of Man are to be considered part of England

  2. - partially published in England

EXAMPLE: The first two pages of a magazine may contain news of a local English church. This may be the only locally printed part of the magazine. The rest of the periodical may consist of a Scottish religious publication.
1.3.5: Non-English Periodicals 

The directory also includes publications which have a specific (dominating) English content, though they may not be published in England. The directory includes those periodicals:

  1. - aimed at English emigrants to the United States

    EXAMPLE:        Main Title :         St. Nicholas            [-- published in NY but read by English children]

ii. - containing news of one of the English border counties, though published in Scotland.

1.4.2: Duplicate(?) Titles 
  • Take care with what appear to be duplicate titles.

    They may be the same title but different publications because published in different towns or at different dates.

    They may be the same title and same publication but:
         -a different edition for a different city;
         -a change from title A to title B and then back to title A.
         -different dates, with a lapse of publication for several issues or even years.

All these distinctions are important to record. If unsure, KEEP TITLES SEPARATE and make notes in the Query field.

EXAMPLES:

Main Title  : British Medical Journal
NbrVolDate : 1832 - 1912+
     ***
Main Title  : British Medical Journal
NbrVolDate : 1842 - 1955
Query : Related to <I>British Medical Journal</I> of 1832?

1.5: General Rules 
1.5.1: Recording Dates 
  • Dates are always recorded in the obvious 4-digit format (ie. DO NOT SHORTEN).

    EXAMPLE: Size : 20cm, 16pp (1865); 25cm, 8pp (1866 - 1880)
    • Separate two or more distinct dates with a comma and space.

      EXAMPLE: Price : 3d (1845, 1867, 1876); 2d (1857, 1871)
    • Dashes within dates should have a space on either side: (1885 - 1887) rather than (1885-1887).
    • Dates are not necessary if it is apparent that an item of information for a periodical does not change during its run. It is assumed that no mention of dates indicates the information exists for the entire run of the periodical.

      EXAMPLE: Publisher : Simpkin, Marshall and Kent

      [if the only publishers for the full run]
    • If entering new information into an entry that already exists, but no dates are indicated, check the
      • Shelf Checked field to see if it has been shelf checked and use the dates of the issues checked (chances are the data were obtained during the shelf check, and hence can be added for clarification).

      EXAMPLE: [This information exists already (in a hypothetical file):]

    Price : 3d
    Shelf Checked : LO/N38 A BB 18 Jul 89 (7:24 Jul 1865)

    [A secondary source states that the above title sold for 2d in 1880. Organize the information like so:]

    Price : 3d (1865); 2d (1880)

    NOTE: If the dates happen to be the same, record anyway and make some mention in the Query field. There may be an error, or the price may refer to different dates within the same year.

    • If the existing entry has not been shelfchecked, then either indicate your dated information after the undated information, or mention the new information and the source taken from in the Query field. If confusion is likely to result, or if the information is of relative importance or is vastly different, indicate so in the Query field.


      • EXAMPLES:

    Size : 16cm, 12pp; 40cm, 2pp (1898)
    Your Queries : Note the difference in size information.
    ***
    Price : 6d
    Your Queries : Stratman lists price as 4d.

    Information within a field is ordered chronologically; that is, the earliest information comes first.

    EXAMPLE:

    Price : 3d (1832); 2d (1865 - 1871); 4d (1872); 6d (1873+)

    • Do not use abbreviated forms in CMNTS field, instead write: Thursday September 1st, 1875.
    • Avoid overpunctuation; strive for clarity and simplicity.

      EXAMPLES:

    Publisher : Cassell & Co Ltd (London 1834)

    [RATHER THAN:]

    Publisher : Cassell, & Co., Ltd. (London, 1834)

    NOTE: See rules for individual fields if unclear.

    2. Title 
    2.1: Definition 

    The title is the name given to the periodical as it appears on the title page and/or top or bottom of the pages within the periodical. The title may not appear within the periodical; it is rare for it to not be on the title page.

    2.2: Formatting Rules 
    2.2.1: Introductory Notes 
    • The title begins with the first word other than an article (the, a, an). Any initial article, including a foreign language article (le, la, l’, der, das, Y,...), is placed after the title and is separated from it by a comma and a space. The article is capitalized.

      EXAMPLES:

    Main Title : Kaleidoscope, The
                      ***
    Main Title : Word With Punch, A
                      ***
    Main Title : Figaro, Le
                      ***
    Main Title : Fledermaus, Die
                      ***
    Main Title : Bedyddiwr, Y
                      ***
    Main Title : Ambigu, L’
    2.2.2: Capitalization 

    Capitalize all words except conjunctions, articles, and prepositions of less than four characters. Even these are capitalized when in the initial position.

    EXAMPLES:

    Main Title : University of London Magazine
                         ***
    Main Title : Manchester County Examiner and Local Reporter, The
                         ***
    Main Title : Man About Town
                         ***
    Main Title : Man in the Moon
                         ***
    Main Title : My Weekly Prize Paper
                         ***
    2.2.3: Punctuation 

    Record punctuation as it appears on the title page, BUT do not use punctuation at the end of the title.

    EXAMPLES:

    Main Title : ‘Eureka’ Monthly Diary
    ***
    Main Title : Comrades: The Boys’ Champion Paper
    ***
    Main Title : "Lady" Catalogue of Fashions
    ***
    Main Title : Beeton’s Boys’ Annual
    (Record apostrophes in the exact location they are found eg. Boy’s vs. Boys’).

    EXCEPTIONS:

    • If the periodical has a subtitle, a semicolon should be placed at the end of the main title field.

    EXAMPLE:

    Main Title : Ainsworth’s Magazine;
    Sub Title : a monthly miscellany of romance, literature, and art

    • If the title contains an acronym, do not use periods between the initials, even if that is how the title appears on the title page.

             EXAMPLE:

                       Main Title : NAPSS [1870] (not N.A.P.S.S., ) (also notice that there are no spaces between the letters)

    • If the title ends with a question mark or exclamation mark.

    EXAMPLES:

    Main Title : Eureka!
    Main Title : What Is It?
    2.2.4: Bound Annual Volumes 

    A periodical (usually weekly or monthly) may be bound in annual volumes under a slightly different name. Separate entries for both the weekly journal and the annual compilation are necessary, with comments within each denoting their relationship.

    EXAMPLE:

    Main Title : Boy’s Annual
    NbrVolDate : vol 1, 1845 - vol 5, 1849
    Comments : This is the annual bound compilation of <I>The Boy’s Weekly</I>.
                                    ***
    Main Title : Boy’s Weekly
    NbrVolDate : vol 1 no 1, 05 Jan 1844 - vol 5 no 52, 29 Dec 1848
    Comments : Bound annually in volumes as <I>The Boy’s Annual</I>.
    2.2.5: Issue vs Volume Title 

    Some periodicals include dates - reflective of the date the issue was released - as part of the title. If this is the case, the earliest date should be recorded in the Title field, enclosed in square brackets [ ].

    EXAMPLES:

    Main Title : London Directory for [1828]
                         ***
    Main Title : Monthly Diary for [Mar 1825], The
    2.2.6: Foreign Language Titles 

    If a publication’s title is in a foreign language (Gaelic, French, German, etc.) the entry should appear under that foreign title even if an English translation of the title is available. The translated English title, if known, should be see-referenced.

    EXAMPLES:

    Main Title : Impartial de Londres, L’
    See Reference : Impartial of London, The

    EXCEPTIONS: Titles whose language contains special characters that the computer cannot type (eg. Hebrew, Greek, Chinese, Russian) should not be entered into the computer. Such titles should be handwritten (any translations known should be noted) and submitted to your supervisor.

    If in doubt as to the wording/appearance of the title, explain clearly in the Your Queries field. In some instances, making a see reference may be helpful.

    EXAMPLES:

    Main Title : Bill of the Play Entertainment World, The
    Your Queries     : Stratman has title as simply <I>Bill of the Play</I>.

    [In this case, making a see reference would be helpful for someone who is trying to find this periodical by either title, therefore:]

    See Reference : Bill of the Play
    2.2.7: Special Cases 

    If ‘St.’ appears in the title (e.g. St. Rollox and Springburn Express), do not change it to ‘Saint’. Similarly do not change ‘Saint’ to ‘St.’

    The abbreviation ‘R.’ for Royal used by some secondary sources should be written in full. Do not treat ‘Royal’ as an article. For example, where BUCOP has:

    EXAMPLES:

    Main Title : Society of Meterological Observations, R.

    [IS CHANGED TO]

    Main Title : Royal Society of Meterological Observations

    If the abbreviation for the word "Company" (Co.) appears in the title, it should be followed by a period (ex. Kelly and Co.'s Directory, not Kelly and Co's Directory).

    If the title contains initials, each initial should be followed by a period, but there should be no spaces between them  (e.g. T.J. and J. Smith's Post Diary).

    If the title contains an acronym, each letter in the acronym should not be followed by a period, and should contain no spaces (e.g. NAPSS [1870]).
    2.2.8: Secondary Phrases 

    The title page will usually record the subtitle. Care must be taken in distinguishing what is considered the title with what is the subtitle. The following ‘rules’ help determine the difference between the two. For our purposes, information that appears immediately after the title shall be referred to as the secondary phrase.

    If the secondary phrase begins with and, or, or of, is it recorded with the main title. The phrase for [year] should also be included as part of the main title.

    EXAMPLES:

    Main Title : Brackley Observer and Northamptonshire, Bucks and Oxon Advertiser
                            ***
    Main Title : Liberal Review of Politics, Society, Literature and Art
                            ***
    Main Title : National Omnibus; and Entertaining Advertiser
                            ***
    Main Title : Voice of Truth or the Baptized Believer’s Penny Magazine
                            ***
    Main Title : Hardwicke’s Shilling Knightage for [1857]

    If the secondary phrase begins with a, the, containing, with which, then record it as the subtitle.

    EXAMPLES:

    Main Title : Jersey Amusements and Visitor’s Guide;
    Sub Title : a weekly programme of coming events

    Note that the word for is not an indication of a title, as in the following examples.

    EXAMPLES:

    Main Title : Belle Isle Home Words;
    Sub Title : for home and hearth
                              ***
    Title : Birkenhead Guardian;
    Sub Title : for Birkenhead, Seacombe, Tranmere, New Brighton

    The physical appearance of the title and secondary phrase(s) on the periodical itself can be a deciding factor. A large size and/or type difference between title and secondary phrase would tend to indicate more of a subsidiary nature on behalf of the latter, and would more likely be a subtitle. Likewise, if the title and secondary phrase are similar in size and/or type, it may lean towards being included as part of the title.

    NOTE: This rule applies to cases where the distinction is not immediately obvious.

    Usually a subtitle is of a descriptive nature, whereas a secondary phrase that should be included with the title can often stand on its own as a title.
    2.2.9: Miscellaneous Notes and Tips 
    • Note that often a periodical, when received for shelf checking, may not contain the title pages. Occasionally the title on the title page may differ slightly with the ‘running title’ (see below). If this occurs, record the title from the title page and indicate the variation in the Comments field.
    • The earliest known form of the title is recorded in this field, even if it is before 1800.
    • Record the title as specifically, accurately and completely as possible.
    • If ‘St.’ appears in the title (e.g. St. Rollox and Springburn Express), do not change it to ‘Saint’. Similarly do not change ‘Saint’ to ‘St.’
    • A ‘running title’ is occasionally used, and is found on the top or bottom of each page. It is often an abbreviation of the main title. Record these in the comments and create a see reference.
    • A ‘wrapper title’ occasionally appeared printed on the wrapping or covering of a single paper or bundle of newspapers. This is usually an abbreviation and should also be recorded in the comments section and then created as a see reference.
    3. Subtitle 
    3.1: Formatting Rules 

    DEFINITION

    Subtitles are phrases which follow titles. They generally indicate one or more of the following items:
    • subject(s) of the periodical
    • contents or description of the periodical
    • clientele or persons to whom the periodical is circulated
    • place(s) of distribution
    • merged publications
    3.1.1: Introductory Notes 
    • Subtitles are to be recorded entirely in lower case, except for proper nouns.
      • EXAMPLE:

    Sub Title : a monthly journal for the county of Lancashire

    • Do not drop any punctuation. Subtitles are never inverted to allow for articles.
      • EXAMPLE:

    Sub Title : a weekly journal of literature, science and the arts

    • If the periodical has a subtitle, a semicolon should be placed at the end of the Title field.
      • EXAMPLE:

    Main Title : Ainsworth’s Magazine;
    Sub Title : a monthly miscellany of romance, literature, and art
    3.1.2: Subtitle Added 
    • If the first subtitle was added after the first issue of the periodical, the subtitle should be appear in quotation marks in the comments field followed by the appropriate dates.

    EXAMPLE:

    Comments : The subtitle "an arts review" was added after vol 3.
    3.1.3: Change in Subtitle 

    Subsequent changes in subtitle are also mentioned in Comments along with the appropriate dates.

    EXAMPLE:

    Comments : Variant subtitles include: "a monthly advertiser" (01 Jun 1880-19 May 1882) and "a monthly advertiser and gazette for Birmingham" (20 May 1882-14 Jul 1884).
    3.1.4: Multiple/Long Subtitles 1 

    A periodical can have more than one subtitle. If the information following the title discusses varying aspects of the periodical, or is very long and seems to have a break in flow (punctuation may be an indicator, especially a period, colon, or semi-colon), then indicate the start of a new subtitle with a semicolon.

    EXAMPLES:

    Main Title : Reciter’s Magazine, The;
    Sub Title : a monthly journal of elocutionary literature; embodied in the form of original and select readings and recitations, in pathos and humour, for private circulation among all who read and recite in public
                         ***
    Main Title : Mother, The;
    Sub Title : a journal of social science; containing useful hints, interesting tales, and other matter, especially calculated to render home happy and healthy children

    NOTE: Generally, avoid more than 2 or 3 lines of text in the SubTitle field.

    3.1.5: Multiple/Long Subtitles 2 

    Occasionally a periodical may have a good deal of information on the title page that could be considered the subtitle. The Comments field may be used to continue especially long Subtitles. See also 3.4d.

    EXAMPLE:

    Main Title : Bradshaw’s Monthly Descriptive Railway Guide and Illustrated Hand-Book of England, Wales, Scotland, and Ireland;

    Sub Title : with splendid travelling maps of Great Britain and Ireland; (see comments)

    Comments : Subtitle continues: "and local maps for tourists of the English and Scottish Lake Districts, North Wales, the Isles of Wight and Man, and Channel Islands; including plans of the following cities and towns: - London, the environs of London, Dublin, Edinburgh, Glasgow, Liverpool, Manchester, Leeds, Birmingham, Sheffield, Bristol, Hull, Oxford, &c., &c., - and embellished with numerous and beautiful steel engravings, illustrative of the magnificent scenery of the British Isles."
    3.1.6: Miscellaneous Notes and Tips 
    • Subtitles are usually located on the title page or title head of the periodical. Note that a periodical may not necessarily have a subtitle, and that many secondary sources often do not indicate subtitles that do exist.
      •
    • Then titles can have subtitles.

    EXAMPLE:

    Then Title : Belgravia; a London magazine
    4. NbrVolDate (Run Numbering) 
    4.1: Formatting Rules 

    DEFINITION

    In primary research, the run numbers are usually found on the title/cover page. For newspapers they are usually below and to the left or right of the title.
    4.1.1: Introductory Notes 
    • All information of this type is to be recorded, using these abbreviations and format rules:

    vol = volume

    s = series (eg. 2s)

    [ns] = new series

    no = number
    • Do not use commas in run numbers, even if a number is greater than 1000.

                             EXAMPLE: vol 89 no 12349    [NOT vol 89 no 12,349]

    • DO use commas to separate volume and number information from the date.

                            EXAMPLE:  vol 3 no 52, 12 Oct 1887    [NOT vol 3 no 52 12 Oct 1887]

     
    4.1.2: Run Numbers 

      ·         Publications can be numbered in one of several ways.  Most periodicals are numbered by volumes, numbers, or both.  Record all run number information provided by primary or secondary sources.

    Abbreviations are not followed by punctuation.

    EXAMPLE:   vol 1 -  vol 17 no 12   

    EXAMPLE:   no 1 [2s] - no 12 [2s]

     

    However, commas should be used to separate volume and number information from the date.

                EXAMPLE:  no 1, 12 Oct 1850    [NOT no1 12 Oct 1850]
    4.1.3: Dates 
    • Days always have two digits, ie. 1 = 01.
    • Months are abbreviated to the first three letters. The first letter is capitalized.

    EXAMPLE: July = Jul ; September = Sep ; June 1st = 01 Jun

    • One space, but no punctuation should be used to separate the parts of the date, but there should be a comma separating the date from the volumes and numbers.

    EXAMPLE: 13 Oct 1876      [NOT 13 Oct, 1876]

    EXAMPLE: vol 1 no 4, 13 Aug 1876     [NOT vol 1 no 4 13 Aug 1876]

            ·     If the date contains a dash, there should be a space between it and the dates surrounding it.

                              EXAMPLE:  08 Nov 1800 - 05 Dec 1804    (rather than 08 Nov 1800-05 Dec 1804).
    4.1.4: Discontinued Periodicals 
    • When a periodical is known to have ceased publication on a certain date, we indicate this by following the ending date by two slashes (//). This information is often written on the top of the last issue itself, or is found in secondary sources.

    EXAMPLE: vol 1 no 1, Jun 1824 - vol 12 no 6, Dec 1836//

    NOTES: DO NOT record the above unless the demise and date are certain (use the Your Queries or Comments fields to indicate any confusing aspects). Discontinuation information does not get recorded in the Comments field, unless the circumstances of the periodical’s demise are known.
    4.1.5: Main Title Run Numbers vs Then Title Run Numbers 
    • When a then title exists, the ending date for the main title will be the date of the issue prior to the name change.

    EXAMPLE:

    Main Title:      Record
    Nbr Vol Date: 1877 - Jun 1886
    Then:               Daily Record. Jul 1886 - 1895
    4.1.6: Unknown Dates 
    • If dates of a nineteenth century publication are unknown use 18??. You may want to make a note in the Query field in addition to this.

      EXAMPLE:

    Main Title:       Daily Star, The
    Nbr Vol Date:  vol 1, 18?? – vol, 1857

    • If the date of the name change is unknown, indicate a question mark.

    EXAMPLE:

    Main Title:      Record, The
    Nbr Vol Date: 1877 - ?
    Then:               Daily Record. ? - 1895

    • When the first or last date or issue number is unknown, the earliest or latest figure available is used.
    • However, if you know the publication expired sometime in the 20th century, but are not sure of the date insert ‘1900+’ as the ending date.

    EXAMPLE:

    Main Title: Record, The
    Nbr Vol Date: vol 1 no 1, Jan 1877 - 1900+
    4.1.7: Doubtful Information 
    • In case of doubt about either the date or the issue number, a question mark is placed after the date. No space(s) should separate the date and the question mark.

    EXAMPLE: vol 7, 1822?
    4.1.8: Approximate Dates 
    • Where an approximate date is known, precede the date with ‘c.’ (circa) instead of a question mark. No space separates the c. from the date.

    EXAMPLE: c.May 1897
    4.1.9: Annual Periodicals 
    • A publication may be published in one year but pertains to the previous or following year (as almanacs or sporting guides often do). Watch for these and be careful to try and indicate the date of PUBLICATION in the Nbr field.

    EXAMPLES:

    Main Title:      London Directory for [1828], The
    Nbr Vol Date: no 1, 1829
                          ***
    Main Title:     Football Guide for [1865], The
    Nbr Vol Date: 1864
    4.1.10: Parallel Numbering 
    • An issue may be numbered in more than one way (e.g. from the beginning of both the year and of publication). Indicate this in the Nbr field within square brackets, or in the Miscellaneous Notes field if space does not allow.

    EXAMPLES:

    Nbr Vol Date: vol 1 no 1, Jan 1889 - vol 2 no 1 [no 13] - Dec 1890//
                              ***
    Nbr Vol Date:                vol 1 part 1, Jan 1876 - vol 2 part 5, May 1877;
                                            vol 1 no 1 [2s] - Jun 1877
    Miscellaneous Notes : Issue 1:1[2s] is also numbered vol 2 part 6.
    4.1.11: Series 
    • Series numbers should always be in this form: [2s], [3s], [4s], etc. Thus s2  becomes [2s] (if the new series is KNOWN to be the second). If a periodical has several new series, the earliest of these becomes [2s], the next one [3s], and so on. Separate the dates for each series with a semicolon when the beginning and ending dates for each are known.

    EXAMPLE:

    vol 2 no 12, Dec 1887 - vol 1 no 1 ns , Jan 1888; vol 1 no 8 ns, Aug 1888
                                             [CHANGE TO:]
    vol 2 no 12, Dec 1887; vol 1 no 1 [ns], Jan 1888 - vol 1 no 8 [ns], Aug 1888

    NOTE: Since ns can refer to the 2nd, 3rd, 4th,.. series, do not change [ns] to [2s] unless you are sure from the information at hand that it is indeed the second series. Otherwise, leave as [ns].
    4.1.12: Registration Issues 
    • If the publication has one or more unnumbered registration issues or prospectus’, the date(s) of these issues should be noted in the comments field only.

    EXAMPLE:      Comments: A registration issue is dated July 5, 1899.

    • However, if the first few numbers were numbered registration issues, the volume and number and date should appear in the run numbers field and a note explaining that, for example, 1:1-1:3 were registration numbers, should appear in the comments field. In this case, the registration issues begin the periodical run.
    4.1.13: Anomalies 
    • Some periodicals provide irregular run number information (ie. not vol:no).
    • Some publications are issued seasonally. Do not capitalize the seasons. Use capitalization for holidays.

    EXAMPLES:         Nbr Vol Date: spring 1887 - Christmas 1890

    • An issue may have a compound date (eg. 1868-1869, or in cases of double issues, 21-28 Dec 1889). Enter both dates, separating them with a slash. Again, no spaces should interrupt the information.

    EXAMPLES:         Nbr Vol Date: vol 1 no 1, 1868/1869 - vol 1 no 3, 21/28 Dec 1889

    • Other types of anomalies that have been found include:

    EXAMPLES:

    Nbr Vol Date: part 1
    Nbr Vol Date: rhif 2
    Nbr Vol Date: series 3
    Nbr Vol Date: 3e année
    Nbr Vol Date: 3rd edition
    Nbr Vol Date: cyf 2
    Nbr Vol Date: 12th year
    Nbr Vol Date: jahrg 3
    Nbr Vol Date: 2nd issue
    Nbr Vol Date: anno [or año] 1
    4.1.14:  

     
    4.1.15: Dashes 

    Spaces are always  used before and after a dash in the dates  in all fields except for the 3 fields: of

    Sources, Histories and Location fields where  there is no space between the dates.
    5. Issuing Bodies 
    5.1: Definition 

    ·         This field will indicate the group, society, club or institution by whom or for whom the periodical was issued (different from the publisher).

    ·         This is an important category, as it may provide a link to related titles, and will often give an indication of the contents of the periodical.  Not all periodicals are affiliated with an issuing body; those that are will often indicate such on the title page (it often forms part of the title - see Generic Title and below).  Sometimes the issuing body, though present, may not be indicated.

    ·         Sometimes, not often, the issuing body will also be the publisher.  For example, Cambridge University may issue a biology journal, and also publish that journal.

    ·         See also the chapter on sub-issuing bodies.
    5.2: Formatting Rules 
    5.2.1: Introductory Notes 
    ·         Issuing bodies often have committees directly responsible for the publication of the periodical.

    These committees should not be listed in the issuing bodies field but rather the sub-issuing body field.  See chapter on sub-issuing body for formatting rules.

    ·         Issuing body names with capitals and initial articles are retained.  There are 2 boxes in the issuing bodies field.  The dates for when the issuing body issued a periodical are indicated in the second box.

     Example:

    New Iss Bodies:

    []

     

    New Iss Bodies:

    ()

     

    ·       Dates should have spaces around the dash, as shown above (i.e. NOT 1812-1830, but 1812 - 1830).
    5.2.2: Issuing Body vs Publisher 

    ·         The difference between an issuing body and a publisher is sometimes unclear.  A publishing company exists primarily for issuing/producing copies of works while an issuing body is a group which has a distinct purpose (such as scientific research or abolishing slavery) and issues a publication only as a secondary function.
    5.2.3: Issuing Body with a Generic Title 
    • If a journal has a generic title, such as Quarterly Notes or Annual, then the issuing body is also listed as the main title.  Generic titles are never main titles.
    5.2.4: Multiple Issuing Bodies 
    • If a title has more than one issuing body enter the name and dates (if available) for one issuing body then save the updates.  Once the data has been saved another set of empty boxes will appear to accept yet another change of issuing body.
    5.2.5: Change Case Only  

    Issuing bodies and people's names need to have <CCO> added to the end of them if we are going to make a change from lower case to capital.   Unless this is done, the software will not recognize the change.

    Example:

     Smithonian religious institute  - would need to be changed to

    Smithsonian Religious Institute<CCO>
    6. Start and End Dates 
    6.1: Note 

    These fields will be suppressed for the reader, but are used for computer data search.
    6.1.1: Start Date 
    • The start date is the first year indicated in the Nbr Vol Date field that the periodical began or the earliest known year.  This and the end date are generated automatically from data in the Nbr Vol Date field, and from the first line in the Comments field.  The start and end dates are the dates that will be listed in brackets next to the main title in a search or browse list.  If our sources provide as a first date only that of some issue other than the first, then that date may appear as the start date.  However, in such cases we sometimes have been able to extrapolate the actual start date, and placed it in the first line of the Comments field, preceded by the phrase "Probably started..."
    • Because the auto-generate program may not be accurate, you are advised to check the entire record to verify the start date.

    Example:       Nbr Vol Date:        no 1, 1813 - no 15, 1813

                          Start Date:        1813 

    Example:       Nbr Vol Date:        1808 - no 15, 1813

                          Start Date:        1808

    Example:        Nbr Vol Date:         no 27 vol 3, 1840 - no 45 vol 4,  Jul 1842

                         Start Date:           1840

     
    6.1.2: End Date 
    • The end date is the last year indicated in the Nbr Vol Date field.  The start and end dates are the dates that will be listed in brackets next to the main title in a search or browse list.

    Example:       Nbr Vol Date:        no 1, 18 Sep 1836 - no 9, 14 Nov 1836

                           End Date:         1836

     

    Example:       Nbr Vol Date:        vol 1, Oct 1889 - vol 11, Dec 1900

                              Then 1:         Lancashire Faces and Places. vol 12 no 1, Jan 1901 - no 10, Oct 1901

                              Then 2:          Faces and Places.  vol 13, Dec 1901 - vol 17 no 2, Feb 1906

                            End Date:         1900

    • In records where there are no then titles, sometimes one or more fields may suggest dates later than in the numbering field.  This may mean that we lack accurate numbering data.  This may happen in the locations field where we indicate a library holds issues to, say, 1842, whereas our numbering data ends at 1841; or it may happen in the comments or people fields. 

    For example, we may list an editor for the years 1872 – 1879, whereas our numbering data ends in 1877.  In these cases ensure that the highest date is in the end date field (but only for records without then titles).

  • When automatically generated, the end date is more likely to be in error (i.e. check it's accuracy) than the start date, simply because it is difficult to know whether the last issue held in the library listed in our Locations field is in fact the last issue published.  We cannot easily resolve this problem, so we wait for further information.  Also, the computer sometimes picks up on the publishing dates of a source listed in the locations field, which date has nothing whatsoever to do with the date of publication of the periodical itself.

    7. Then Titles 
    7.1: Definition 

    ·         This field is designed to record changes in title.  If during the course of the periodical’s existence, a change in the title occurs, the new title along with the appropriate run numbers are recorded in the Then Title field.

    ·    There is no need to record very many changes in title in the twentieth century; however, the rules for how many to record are not very well defined.  In general, if a periodical changes its name up to five times between 1900 and 1950, record all of those changes, but none after 1950 (except perhaps in the comments field).  On the other hand, if a periodical only changes its name twice in the twentieth century, once in 1940 and once in 1980, both of those changes could be recorded, even though the second is after 1950.

    ·    Only the title, date and place information for titles in the 1900's should be recorded (i.e. do not record editors, proprietors, etc. for the twentieth century).

    ·         Sometimes a title will change, then change back to its original form.

    ·         Sometimes a title will change as the result of a merger.  (Eg. Newcastle Times may absorb the Newcastle Miners News to become the Newcastle Times and Miner’s News).
    7.2: Formatting Rules 
    7.2.1: Introductory Notes 

    ·         The title begins with the first word other than an article (the, a, an). Any initial article, including a foreign language article (le, la, l’, der, das, Y,...), is placed after the title and is separated from it by a comma and a space.  The article is capitalized and is followed by a period.

                    EXAMPLE:       Then:    Calvinistic Pulpit, The.

    ·         Leave two spaces after the period that ends the then title, before placing the run numbers.

                    EXAMPLE:       Then:    Calvinistic Pulpit, The.  vol 1 no 1, 01 Jan 1891 - vol 5 no 12, 01 Dec 1895

    ·         In the two small boxes at the end of the then title field, repeat the start and end year of the then title.  These dates will be the dates listed in brackets next to the title in search and browse lists.

                    EXAMPLE:       Then:    Calvinistic Pulpit, The.  vol 1 no 1, 01 Jan 1891 - vol 5 no 12, 01 Dec 1895  {1891 - 1895}
    7.2.2: Then Titles with Subtitles or Generic Titles 
         ·         An then title can have a subtitle or a generic title.

            EXAMPLES:     Then:    Weekly Recorder, The; the leading newspaper of Stoke Newington, South Hornsey, etc.  no 898, 01 Feb 1889 - no 931, 13 Sep 1889//

                                               Then:    Proceedings of the Ethnological Society of London.  no 1, 1850 - no 3, 1851 

    ·       If it is not clear how the generic title fits in with the then title (i.e. in your information, you have simply Ethnological Society of London as the title, and the fact that it was the proceedings), then you would enter the society name, followed by a period and two spaces, followed by the generic title.

      EXAMPLE:        Then:    Ethnological Society of London.  Proceedings.  no 1, 1850 - no 3, 1851 

    ·         A change in generic title qualifies as a change in then title.

                    EXAMPLE:       Then 1:    Proceedings of the Central Chamber of Agriculture

                                                          Then 2:    Journal of the Central Chamber of Agriculture
    7.2.3: See References 
     ·         All then titles are automatically displayed in browse and search lists.  It is no longer necessary to create see references for them, and any see references for then titles currently in existence should be deleted.  See references for parts of then titles, however, should still remain.  Also, variations in spelling of a then title should be see references as well. 

    Follow these steps to make a see reference: 

    1.      On the edit record page, add then title and save.

    2.      Select  Edit See Refs located on the menu at the top of the page.

    3.      Enter any necessary see references from the them title in the box labelled New See Reference.  (For example, if your then title was Halfpenny Picture Magazine for Little Children, you would want to create a see reference for "Picture Magazine for Little Children).  Do not place a period at the end of the see reference, nor quotation marks around it.

    4.      Click Save Changes to finish.
    8. Generic Titles 
    8.1: Definition 
    Many titles begin with generic title form, such as journal, annual report, etc..  In order to avoid the cluttering of such titles, they are to be indexed under the respective issuing body, with the generic title indicated in the Generic Title field.

    TYPES OF GENERIC TITLES:

                            Journal                               Annals

                            Transactions                     Memoirs

                            Proceedings                      Annual/Monthly Reports

                            Report                                Minutes

    Exception: 

    ·         A title may begin with a generic title which is not followed by an issuing or sub-issuing body.  These titles should be recorded as found.

                    EXAMPLE:   Main Title:    Annals of Science

                                                    Main Title:    Journal of Anthropology
    8.2: How to Format Generic Titles 
    ·         If the publication is the ‘official organ of’ (or ‘supported by’) an issuing body, and has a generic title, the issuing body name is recorded in the main title field as well as the issuing bodies field.

                    EXAMPLE:   Main Title:    Church of England, The

                                       Issuing Bodies:    Church of England

                                       Generic Title:    Proceedings of 

    ·         The first word in the generic title field is capitalized as well as all proper nouns.

    ·         Record the generic title in the generic title field as it appears on the periodical, including all conjunctions, prepositions, etc, that may follow or precede.

                    EXAMPLE: [Title page has: The Journal of the Church of England]

                                            Main Title:             Church of England, The
                                            Generic Title:          Journal of, The

    ·         If there are several generic titles for the same issuing body, enter the first generic title then click save at the bottom of the page.  A refreshed page will display with the first generic title entered accompanied by a blank box for entering another generic title.  This process can be repeated as often as needed.  For each generic title, the date and numbering information, if known, should be entered beside the generic title, preceded by a period and two spaces, and enclosed in brackets.  Also, any names (contr, pubr, print, edtr, etc.), sizes, departments, frequencies, prices, illustration info, or indexing info that are associated with a particular generic title, but not with all of the others, should have a shortened form of the generic title included in parentheses after the information.

                    EXAMPLE:
                                               Generic Title:            Journal of.  (no 1, 1841 - no 6, 1847)
                                                                                  Proceedings of, The.  (vol 1 no 1, 1838 - vol 3 no 5, 1844)

                                                   Editors:              Smith, James (jour 1845); Sampson, Jill (proc 1842)
                                                Indexing:              T of C/no (jour); list of names/no (proc)
                                        Departments:               letters to the editor, news of the week, poetry (jour
                                                                              1842); news of the week, sports, society news (proc)

    • NOTE that there should be a space on either side of the dash in dates in generic titles (no 1, 1841 - no 6, 1847, NOT no 1, 1841-no 6, 1847).
    9. Place of Publication 
    9.1: Definition 

     ·         In this field, the cities or towns of publication are noted, along with the dates which the periodical was published there.  This information is most often found with the publishing/printing information in the periodical.

    ·         The English directory consists of publications which were published in England (including Guernsey, Jersey and Isle of Man).  There are, however, some exceptions to this rule.  Check an atlas or www.genuki.org.uk to ensure the place of publication is in England.  If it is not, ask someone whether or not this place is one of the exceptions.
    9.2: Counties 

    ·        Include county information for all towns.  This is important as in several instances two or more towns share the same name.

                    EXAMPLES:     Place: Dorchester (Dorset)
    Place: Dorchester (Oxfordshire)
    9.3: London Districts and Regions 
     ·        Sometimes the city suburb or district where the periodical was published is indicated.   For London, the street, if a common one, may also be indicated.  Follow the formatting example online for the place of publication field and enter the street name in the brackets where the date would go.           

                    EXAMPLES:     Place: London, London (Fleet Street)

     ·         Regions of London are formatted like this in the browse list and in the view mode within a record:

    EXAMPLE:       Place: London W.C. (London)

    [when entering this as a new place of publication (i.e. in the edit mode), you would format it as: London W.C., London (date)]

    • Abbreviations of some regions:    W. - West
                                                              E. - East
                                                         N. - North
                                                         S. - South
                                                     W.C. - West Central
                                                      E.C. - East Central
                                                     N.W. - North West

     Watch for these abbreviations - the periodical may not state ‘London’, but indicate (eg.) W.C. instead, which refers to the West Central district of London.
    9.4: Miscellaneous Notes 
     ·         For periodicals published in several places simultaneously, names should be separated by semi-colons followed by appropriate run dates.  (See online database for formatting example provided above place of publication field).

     ·         Do not capitalize pronouns and articles that are part of the place name.

                    EXAMPLES:      Place: Ashby-de-la-Zouch
    Place: Ashton-under-Lyne

     ·         Do not list places where the paper was printed or circulated.  These can be recorded in the Comments field, or in the Subtitle field if it is part of the subtitle.  Remember that the place field is for the place of PUBLICATION.

     ·         Do not include the place names connected with agents of shops or booksellers distributing the publication.

     ·         Change foreign spellings to English.

                    EXAMPLE:       The original document gives place as Edimbourgh.

                                     Place: Edinburgh

     ·         Note variant spellings of places in the same parentheses as the dates.

                    EXAMPLE:       Place : Berwick-upon-Tweed, Northumberland (Berwick)

    ·       Dates should have spaces around the dash [i.e. NOT Edinburgh, Scotland (1812-1830), but

     (1812 - 1830)].
    10. Merge 
    10.1: Merge 

    ·         Sometimes a periodical has a relationship to or an affiliation with another periodical.  Such information is indicated in the MERGE field.
    10.1.1: Formatting Rules 

    ·         Use standard capitalization rules for titles.  Do not use complete sentences (and hence no periods) in this field.  Use semi-colons to separate individual merge information and web quoting <I> </I> to italicise titles online.

                    EXAMPLES:   Merge:   absorbed by New Baptist Magazine (1833)

                                    Merge:   merged with Straggling Astrologer (21 Aug 1824)

                                    Merge:   absorbed Newspaper and Poster Advertising; merged with Advertising News

     ·         Standard terms should be used in this field:

                           ‘absorbed’              - when the main entry paper took over another paper

                           ‘absorbed by’         - when the main entry paper was itself taken over by another paper

                           ‘merged with’        - when two papers combined, often to form a new publication

     

    ·         Some sources will use the terms ‘combined with’, ‘amalgamated with’, ‘incorporated with’.  Quote these sources directly.  These terms may be converted to the three standard terms in a final edit.  Below are some other examples you may run into.

                                EXAMPLES:   Merge:  merged with Bonne Bouche to form Adventurer and Bonne Bouche (1824)

                                             Merge:  incorporated with Academy Notes (1895)

                                             Merge:  amalgamated with The Argus and subsequently published as The Age and Argus

                                             Merge: superseded by Academic Annals

                                             Merge: supersedes Academy Sketches

                                             Merge: subsequently incorporated in Sunday Chronicle Football Annual

                                             Merge: subsequently issued as the Sunday Chronicle Cricket and Golf Annual

    ·         Watch for when a source states ‘continued as’, as this is likely to be a Then title, and should be recorded as such.  If unsure, make a note in the Query field.
    11. Size 
    11.1: Definition 

    This field is used to record information on the physical size of the publication; specifically, the measured length and number of pages.
    11.2: Formatting Rules 
    11.2.1: Introductory Notes 
     ·         Measurements of the length of the publication from top to bottom are given in cm.  Do not separate the number and the abbreviation ‘cm’ for centimetre(s) with a space.  Round off measurements to the next highest whole number.

                    EXAMPLES:   Size: 35cm

                                    Size: 23cm       [-- even if it measures 22½cm]

     ·         The number of pages follows the length information, separated by a comma and a space. If a date is available for the data, it should be the last item of information and should be placed in parentheses.

                 EXAMPLES:   Size: 18cm, 8pp (1899)

                                    Size: 25cm, 32pp

    ·         If a periodical remained the same size, but changed its number of pages, note the size in cm only once.  Likewise, if the size changes, but the number of pages stays the same, do not repeat the size.

                    EXAMPLES:   Size: 28cm, 16pp (1819); 24pp (1820); 30pp (1840)

                                    Size: 16cm, 12pp (1880); 12cm (1882); 12cm, 24pp (1885)

    ·         A periodical may have a separate advertising section (usually found at the end) or an appendix or supplement.  Indicate such information in the Size field, following the initial pagination information and separated by a + sign.

                    EXAMPLES:   Size: 15cm, 4pp + 6pp supp (1899)

                                    Size: 17cm, 137pp + appendix + 9pp adv (1868)

    ·     If the date in brackets contains a dash, there should be a space between it and the dates surrounding it (example: 1800 - 1804, rather than 1800-1804).
    11.2.2: Clipped Pages 

    ·         Often the library copies of periodicals have been clipped or cut off on the tops and/or bottoms so that they fit into their bindings or are even and/or smooth.  Try to watch for this, and adjust your length measurement by adding 2cm, unless it is obvious that more was cut off.
    11.2.3: Variable Pagination/Length 

    ·         A periodical’s pagination or length may vary from issue to issue.  Do not attempt to indicate each and every issue’s measurements, but rather, indicate the range of pages and respective dates.

                    EXAMPLE:     Size: 26-32pp (1888)
    11.2.4: Abbreviations 

    ·         You might encounter the following abbreviations:

                    4pp                      4 pages

                    4to                      quarto (25-30cm)

                    8vo                      octavo (20-25cm)

                    12mo                   duodecimo (17.5-20cm)

                    16mo                   sextodecimo (15-17.5cm)

                    18mo                   octodecimo (12.5-15cm)

                    32mo                   trigesimo-secundo (10-12.5cm)

                    48mo                   quadragesimo-octavo (7.5-10cm)

                    64mo                   sexagesimo-quarto (less than 7.5cm)

    This information is used in the British Library Catalogue, and, in the absence of other information (eg. from shelfcheck or secondary source), this data should be entered as found.

    EXAMPLE:              Size: 8vo, 16pp
    12. Price 
    12.1: Definition 

    ·         Here, the price per issue or volume or the subscription price for a given period of time is recorded.  This information is usually available on the cover page of the periodical.
    12.2: Formatting 
    12.2.1: Abbreviations 

    £              pound (alt + 0163)
    s              shilling
    d              pence
    /vol           per volume
    /m            per month
    /q             per quarter
    /a             per annum (year)
    /d                 per day

    Price information is assumed to be per issue, unless otherwise stated.

                    EXAMPLES:   Price: 3d (1840)                 [-- not 3d/no]

                                    Price: 54s3d/vol (1845 - 1860)

                                    Price: 10s/a         [-- the annual subscription rate is 10 shillings]

                                    Price:  £4 3s/a     [--the annual subscription rate is 4 pounds and 3 shillings]
    12.2.2: Other Currencies 

    ·         The terms ‘crown’ or ‘guinea’ occur less frequently, so they are written in full.  Other types of currency may also appear, especially foreign ones (eg. francs/centimes, dollars/cents, etc..).  These too should be typed in full.

                    EXAMPLES:   Price:  1 crown, 2 guineas/a

                                                         Price:    20 centimes, 2 francs/a

                                    Price:  8d, 25 cents, £2/a, 4 dollars/a
    12.2.3: Gratis Periodicals 

    ·         If a publication such as an advertiser was issued free of charge, we input ‘gratis’.

                    EXAMPLE:     Price: gratis
    12.2.4: Stamped Periodicals 

    ·         Some issues list two prices, an unstamped and a stamped price (the stamped price is usually ½d or 1d more).  Always list the unstamped price first, then the stamped price.

                    EXAMPLE:     Price: 4d, 5d st (1888)    [-- st follows the stamped price]
    12.2.5: Miscellaneous Notes 

    ·         Any extra information relating to price can be placed either in brackets in the price field, if short, or in the comments section.

                    EXAMPLES:   Price: gratis (to members), 3d (to non-members)

    ·         NOTES TO SHELFCHECKERS:

                i.           ‘By post’ refers to the stamped price.

           ii.        A periodical may list several different prices (/vol, /m, /q, /a, cloth bound, with gold lettering, etc..).  It is usually satisfactory to indicate the per issue and per annum prices.  Prices for special types of binding, paper or other features should be noted with discretion as to importance, uniqueness and amount of price information already available (the Cmnts field is also available).  Avoid listing more than 5 prices for one date.

                          EXAMPLE:     Price:  3d, 4d st, 3s/q, 3s6d/q st, 18d/a, 22d/a st, 34d/vol (cloth bound), £5/vol (printed on vellum), £5 6d/vol (gold-lettering on vellum)

                                                     [ONE WAY TO MAKE THIS LESS CLUTTERED:]

                                                     Price :                  3d, 4d st, 18d/a, 22d/a st

                                 Comments :        Also available printed on vellum for £5/vol; for £5 6d, the volume also would feature gold lettering.

    ·     If the date in brackets contains a dash, there should be a space between it and the dates surrounding it (example: 1800 - 1804, rather than 1800-1804).
    12.2.6: Subscription Rates 

    Price may be listed as per issue, or on a subscription basis, which is usually an annual subscription, though sometimes it is quarterly subscription.  Subscription rates should be formatted as follows:

    EXAMPLE: 10s/a (1807) [the yearly subscription was ten shillings in 1807]
                   3s/q  [three shillings per quarterly subscription]

    If both a per issue price and a subscription rate are listed:

    EXAMPLE: 1d, 10s/a  [one pence per issue, ten shillings per yearly subscription]
                  3d, 5s/q (1840)  [the price in 1840 was three pence per issue, or five shillings per quarterly subscription]

    The subscription rates need not be listed extensively.  Use your own discretion as to whether or not the subscription rate is needed.  For example, if the price field already contains extensive per issue prices, the subscription rate can be left out.
    13. Circulation 
    13.1: Circulation 

    Definition:

    This field records the number of issues sold in any given time period.  This information is not usually indicated on the periodical, though it may be found on the title page in rare instances.  Generally, however, most circulation data is located in secondary sources.  Note that the PLACE of circulation is not recorded in this field.
    13.1.1: Formatting Rules 

    ·         The number of issues or time period should follow the circulation figure using the same format as that used for price.

                    EXAMPLES:    Circulation: 5,000/m   [-- DO use commas if the figure is greater than 1000]

                                                          Circulation:   243/no

                                             NOTE: Use the abbreviation /no to clearly indicate that the circulation is per issue.

    ·         Use the same abbreviations used in the price field to indicate the various time periods.

                    EXAMPLES:       Circulation: 1,000/d; 5,000/w; 20,000/m; 60,000/q; 200,000/a

     ·         Circulation is sometimes calculated by using the number of stamps (a kind of tax per copy) issued as a guide.  Record this as follows:

                    EXAMPLE:         Circulation: 50,000 stamps sold (1899)

    ·         Places in which a periodical circulates are mentioned in the comments field (or in the subtitle field if part of the subtitle), but NOT in the circulation field.

                    EXAMPLES:       Subtitle: circulating in the counties of Berks, Bucks and Oxon

                                        Comments:     “Circulates in Alcester and adjacent towns and villages in Warwickshire, Worcestershire, and Gloucestershire” (Mitchell’s 1898).

    ·         Likewise, the persons, or group(s) of persons to whom the paper is circulated should also be indicated in the comments field (or in the subtitle field if part of the subtitle) and NOT in the circulation field.

                    EXAMPLE:         Subtitle:  circulating privately amongst the members of the Amateur Printers’ Association

                                     Comments:     Circulates to the members of the dental profession.

    ·         Treat circulation figures given by publishers of a periodical with scepticism especially when they are linked with advertising rates.  Note the source in the QUERY field if you suspect the circulation figure is incorrect.

                    EXAMPLE:         Circulation:    50,000/no (1853)

                                        Queries:  The circulation figure for 1853 (50,000/no) is from Mitchell’s and may not be accurate.
    14. Frequency 
    14.1: Definition 

    This field indicates the time span between the publication of consecutive issues.  This is most often found on the title page, but if not indicated, a quick check of the dates of several consecutive issues can determine its frequency.
    14.2: Formatting Rules 

    ·         Use the following terms:

                     daily                                     monthly

                    six times weekly                 every two months

                    thrice weekly                      quarterly

                    twice weekly                       thrice annually

                    weekly                                 twice annually

                    monthly                               annually

                    fortnightly                           every two weeks

                    twice monthly                     irregularly

     

    ·         DO NOT USE  the following terms:

                     semiweekly                         biannually

                    biweekly                              biennially

                    bimonthly

     

    ·         Indicate dates for clarification.  Use standard formatting rules (including using spaces around the dash).

                    EXAMPLE:     Frequency: monthly (1822 - 1844); weekly (1845 - 1860); quarterly (1862)

     ·         If the specific day or time of publication within a frequency is known, indicate within brackets.  Indicate also if this changes during the paper’s publication.

                    EXAMPLES:   Frequency:   weekly (Sat 1808 - 1814); monthly (1st day 1815, last day 1816+)

                                   Frequency:   weekly (Sat 1834, Sun 1835)

                                   Frequency:   daily (pm 1825)

                                   Frequency:   fortnightly (Tue 1865); twice monthly (1st and 15th 1866)

                                        Frequency:    twice weekly (Mon Thu 1801 - 1806); thrice weekly (Mon Wed Fri 1867, 1880 - 1882); weekly (Mon 1883; Wed 1884, 1887); monthly (15th 1893)

          NOTE: Days of the week are recorded in three-letter format.
    14.3: Fortnightly vs Twice Monthly 

    ·         Fortnightly means the paper is published every 14 days, whereas twice monthly means 2 issues per month, which comes to every 15-16 days, usually.  Fortnightly papers are published on the same day of the week.  Be sure to apply the correct term.

    15.1: Definition 

    ·         This field is used to record the types of illustrations, if any, that are found within the periodical.
    15.2: Formatting Rules 
    15.2.1: Introductory Notes 

    ·         Use short one or two‑word descriptions.  Follow standard format.

                    EXAMPLES:   Illustration:   woodcuts, colour photographs, lithographs, etchings

                                    Illustration:   engravings, sketches (1876); photographs, engravings, woodcuts, steel lithographs (1886)
    15.2.2: Black and White vs Colour Illustrations 
     ·         Unless otherwise indicated, illustrations are assumed to be in black & white; it is unnecessary to indicate so.  Put ‘b/w’ if needed for clarification.  Indicate if illustrations are in colour.

                    EXAMPLES:   Illustration: photographs (1878); colour photographs (1899)

                                    Illustration: sketches, colour and b/w engravings (1880)
    15.2.3: Extra Information 

    ·         Often, illustrations are found in certain sections of the periodical (e.g. only the title page or only in the advertisements).  This should be indicated as follows:

                    EXAMPLES:   Illustration: photographs (supp only)     [-- supp = a supplement issued with the periodical]

                                    Illustration: sketch (title page only)

                                    Illustration: colour engravings (adv only)
                                                   [-- they appear only in the advertisements]
    15.2.4: Illustrators 

    ·         If the names of the illustrators are known, these should be recorded in the Contributors field, followed by (ill.) to specify their contribution.  If all illustrations were provided by the same person, this should also be recorded in the illustration field.

                    EXAMPLE:     Illustration: engravings (by J. Hatfield)

                                    Contributors: J. Hatfield (ill.)

    17. Indexing 
    17.1: Definitions 

    Indexing

    ·         This field can refer to indexing within a periodical (internal) or to external indexes listing the periodical and/or its contents.  Internal indexes are almost always found either at the beginning or end of the periodical.  External indexes are usually discovered through secondary research.

    Table of Contents (T of C)

    ·         for the purposes of this project, the table of contents lists items in order of appearance within the periodical and is usually located at the beginning of the periodical

    Index

    ·         the index lists items, such as subjects, titles, authors, or other items, in alphabetical order and is usually found at the end of the periodical - an index is sometimes cumulative dealing with several issues or volumes

                    NOTE: Occasionally the term ‘table of contents’ is used interchangeably with the term ‘index’.
    17.1.1: Miscellaneous Notes 
     ·         For internal indexing use the following abbreviations:

              index in each volume = index/vol

          table of contents in each volume = T of C/vol    [-- note spacing - T_of_C]

              index in each issue = index/no

    NOTE: ALWAYS INDICATE if the indexing is /no or /vol (or other); never put just ‘T of C’.

     ·         A periodical may contain both an index and a table of contents.

                    EXAMPLE:       Indexing:  T of C and index/no  [-- or /vol, depending on the case]

     ·         A periodical may have an index or table of contents for each number and volume.

                    EXAMPLE:      Indexing: T of C/no and vol, index/no and vol
    17.1.2: Special Indexes 

    Indicate any special indexes within the periodical (special topics, cumulative indexes, etc..).

            EXAMPLES:   Indexing: names index and place index/vol

                             Indexing: T of C and advertisers index/no

                             Indexing: list of illustrations and advertisers/vol

                             Indexing: index to vols 1-10 (1872-1881) included with vol 11
    17.1.3: External Indexes 

    ·         Include external indexes giving complete bibliographical information.  Use correct format and do not forget to include titles of books within italic web quotings.

                    EXAMPLES:     Indexing: <I>Poole’s Index to Periodicals</I> 1882-1906

                                      Indexing: Index in <I>VPN</I> 1:2 : 27-49
    17.1.4: Order of Information 
    1.     description of index(es) (eg. place, names index, T of C)

    2.     cumulated indexes, if any (with dates)

    3.     publication information of index (place, publisher, date of publication, or, if unpublished, list location of manuscript copy)

                    EXAMPLE:                 Indexing: T of C/no (1846 - 1853); index/vol (1854 - 1870); place index/vols 1‑20 (in vol 20); subject and names index/1820‑1840 in Henry Jones’  <I>The London Habit Magazine</I>. Ph.d. dissertation.  Columbia U, Georgia, 1976.
    17.1.5: Manuscript Copies 
     ·         For cases where the index has not been published, list the location of the manuscript copies.

                    EXAMPLE:     [A small public library may have a birth, marriage, and death index available for a particular local newspaper:]

                                    Indexing: Watford Public Library has b/m/d index
    18. Departments 
    18.1: Definition 
     ·         This field describes the various sections and topics regularly covered in a journal, and is generally taken directly from the table of contents of the periodical.  Where no T of C is available, look for recurrent headings which appear in bold or are underlined.
    18.2: Formatting Rules 

    ·         Advertisements, stories, tales, serial tales, poetry etc. are included in the department field even if such a heading is not found within the publication.

                EXCEPTION:  Miscellaneous should not be included as a department.

    ·         Separate the listings of departments belonging to different issues by adding the date of the issue in parentheses followed by a semicolon at the end of each list.

                    EXAMPLE:     Departments:  advertisements, foreign news, local news (1899); poetry, sports, letters to the editor (1900)

            NOTE: Following regular format, departments of the same date are separated by commas.

    ·         If a word is repeated in several departments (eg. ‘news’, ‘notes’), a shortened form may be used:

                    EXAMPLE:  Departments: editorial, foreign news, domestic news, local news

                                                   [CAN BE ABBREVIATED TO:]

                                 Departments: editorial, foreign/domestic/local news

    ·         Abbreviations: births, marriages and deaths = b/m/d; marriages and deaths = m/d

                    EXAMPLE:     Departments: foreign news, b/m/d (1881); local news, m/d (1883)

    ·     Since the departments field is often made up of titles of sections from the table of contents, sometimes commas within the titles cause a problem.  For example:  If the departments field says: “how to advertise, English Scotch and Irish newspapers, London local papers, magazines trade organs etc., almanacs, provincial daily newspapers, a list of English provincial newspapers, foreign and colonial newspapers (1881)”, you can see that the second item would originally have been “English, Scotch and Irish newspapers”, but the comma after “English” would make “English” look like a separate item from “Scotch and Irish Newspapers”.  So to solve that problem, we would put this into the departments field as “English/Scotch/and Irish newspapers”, using slashes instead of commas.  We would include a slash before the "and" even if there were no comma there in the original to avoid confusion with point 3 above, which uses slashes when words are repeated in several departments.  For the above example, the whole field would say: “how to advertise, English/Scotch/and Irish newspapers, London local papers, magazines trade organs etc., almanacs, provincial daily newspapers, a list of English provincial newspapers, foreign and colonial newspapers (1881).”

    20. Sources 
    20.1: Sources 
     ·         The sources and histories fields record secondary sources which contain information on the periodical entry.  The difference between Sources and Histories is not always clear.  In general, a source is a publication or manuscript which helps to confirm the existence or location of a periodical, while a history provides a description or other background information on a periodical.  Thus a newspaper containing an advertisement for a little‑known magazine would be a source, while an article on a certain magazine in the International Book Lover would make the IBL a history.
    20.1.1: Formatting Rules 

    ·         Page numbers for sources (also for indexing‑‑external indexes‑‑or for histories), p or pp is followed by a period, but no space.

                    EXAMPLES:   Sources: Lefanu (1843), p.128.; Jack (1855), pp.92-94.

    ·         Separate entries with a semi-colon.  Each entry should end with a period.  Titles should be italicized using the appropriate web symbols (<i> </i>).

                    EXAMPLE:     Sources: BUCOP.; Mitchell’s Newspaper Press Directory.
    20.1.2: Dashes 

    There are no spaces used before and after the dash when recording dates  i.e.  14 Feb 1840-30 Nov 1894.

    This rule applies ONLY to Sources, Histories and Location fields
    21. Histories 
    21.1: Definition 
     ·         The sources and histories fields record secondary sources which contain information on the periodical entry.  The difference between Sources and Histories is not always clear.  In general, a source is a publication or manuscript which helps to confirm the existence or location of a periodical, while a history provides a description or other background information on a periodical.  Thus a newspaper containing an advertisement for a little known magazine would be a source, while an article on a certain magazine in the International Book Lover would make the IBL a history.
    21.2: Formatting 
    21.2.1: Rules 

    ·         For an entry in histories and sources fields which has both a volume number and a series number, these should be separated by a colon.

                    EXAMPLE:     Histories: Rayner, N&Q 6:2s (Jun 1965), p.182.

                     In all other cases, series numbers should be in square brackets [2s].

                    EXAMPLE:     Histories: Rayner, N&Q [2s] (Jan 1899), p.185.

                                                [In this case no volume number exists or is given]

    ·         Separate entries with a semi‑colon, and end each entry with a period.  Titles should be italicized using the web symbols <i> and </i>.

                    EXAMPLE:     Histories: Rayner, N&Q [2s] (Jan 1899), p.185.; Mitchell’s Newspaper Press Directory.
    21.2.2: Samples for Recording Bibliographic Material 
    Newspapers

    Histories:       Author, The.  “Title of Article.”  Title of Newspaper.  Day Month Year: edition [if provided], page.

    Journals

    Histories:      Author, The.  “Title of Article.”  Title of Journal [appropriately abbreviated] volume [Arabic numeral only].issue number [if provided] (Month [abbreviated] year): pages.

    Example:      Scott, J.  “Catching Flies With Honey.”  Fly Catcher 43.3 (Apr 1986): 55-68.

    Books

    Histories:       Author, The.  Title of Book.  Ed. His Name.  Series title.  Place: Publisher, Date.

    Chapter in a Book

    Histories:       Author, The.  “Title of Chapter.”  Title of Book.  Ed. His Name. 2 vols. Place: Publisher, date.  page(s).

    Introduction, Preface, or Afterword to a Book

    Histories:       Author, The.  Introduction.  Title of Book.  By The Author.  Place: Publisher, date.  page(s).

    Article in a Reference Work

    Histories:      “Title of article.”  Name of reference work.  Year. vol: page(s).

    Example:        “Alfred Barry”.  Dictionary of National Biography: Supplement, Jan. 1901-Dec. 1911. 1912. I: 103-105.

    Dissertation

    Histories:      Author, The.  “Title of dissertation” (underline the title if the dissertation has been published).  Diss.  University, Date.

    Example:        Boyle, Anthony T. “The Pre-Raphaelites and The Germ.” Diss.  Oxford University, 1945.

    21.2.3: Dashes 

    There are no spaces used before and after the dash when recording dates  i.e.  14 Feb 1840-30 Nov 1894.

    23.3.3: Run Numbers 

    ·         In numbering the issues, use 2:3, not vol 2 no 3.  If there is only a volume or issue number, then use the abbreviations.  Join issue numbers and dates by a single hyphen with no space on either side.

                     EXAMPLES: Location: partial runs: LO/N38 A vol 4‑6:3 (1870‑1872)

                                  Location: partial runs: LO/N38 A nos 1-24 (21 Jan-15 May 1887)

                     EXAMPLES:   Location:   partial runs: OX/U‑1 A vols 1‑10

                                    Location:   partial runs: OX/U‑1 A nos 14‑21
    23.3.4: Series 
     ·         Series, if present, are included in square brackets after the issues to which they refer, separated by a space.

                    EXAMPLE:    Location:   partial runs: LO/N-1 A 1:1 [2s]‑vol 21 [3s] (Jan 1845-1866)
    23.3.5: Dates 

    ·         Do not repeat months or years unnecessarily.

                    EXAMPLES:   Location:   partial runs: LO/N-1 A (May, Jul, Sep 1810)

                                                      [-- NOT: May 1810, Jul 1810, Sep 1810]

                                    Location:   partial runs: LO/U-1 G (Mar-Dec 1887)

                                                       [-- NOT: Mar 1887-Dec 1887]

    ·     Contrary to common practice in most other fields, if the date in brackets contains a dash, there should NOT be a space between it and the dates surrounding it (example: 1800-1804, rather than 1800 - 1804).
    23.3.6: Ending Date Unknown 

    ·         If the ending date or  vol:no for the library’s holdings is unknown or is followed by ‘etc.’, use a plus(+) sign immediately after the last known date or vol:no to denote continuation of the run.

                    EXAMPLES:   Location:    partial runs: LE/U‑1 (1862+)                               [-- not 1862, etc.]

                                    Location:    partial runs: QZ/P99 vols 440+ (1849+)
    23.3.7: Incomplete Holdings 

    ·         Often library holdings are incomplete.  In all cases, a description of the ‘wanting issues’ appear inside the parenthesis after the string of dates which are available.  Separate the wanting issues from those available by using a comma and a space.  Write the word ‘wanting’ out in full.

                    EXAMPLE:     Location:  partial runs: LO/N‑1 A vols 1‑10 (1885‑1895, wanting vols 4‑6)

    ·         Sometimes holdings are so incomplete that an accurate list would take too much space.  In that case simply give the beginning and ending numbers of the period in question followed by the abbreviation ‘inc’ (for incomplete).

                    EXAMPLE:     Location:  partial runs: LO/N38 A vols 1‑6 (1870‑1878 inc, 1880, 1892‑1894)

                                   [-- means that the holdings for 1870‑1878 are not continuous]
    23.3.8: Missing or Damaged Pages 
    ·         Issues in UK libraries may have pages missing, or damaged pages. Where the copy in question is a rare copy, it should be included in the location list with the abbreviation ‘imp’ for imperfect in parenthesis.

                    EXAMPLE:     Location:    partial runs: LO/N38 A no 2 (14 Mar 1859 imp)
    23.3.9: Missing or Destroyed Issues/Runs 

    ·         Sometimes a library’s run may be destroyed or missing.  Note this after the dates, separating with a hyphen and spaces before and after.

              EXAMPLES:   Location:  partial runs: LO/N-1 A nos 1-10 (1824-1833 - destroyed in war)

                               Location:   partial runs: LO/N-1 A (1865-1876 - missing since 1978)

                    NOTE: The British Library had several sections of its holdings bombed during the war.  Record this as shown in the first example above if it applies to the particular periodical.
    23.3.10: Microfilmed Periodicals 

    ·         Libraries might not hold original issues, but microfilmed copies.  The format for an entire microfilmed run is:

                    EXAMPLE:     Location:     partial runs: LO/N-1 A nos 1‑10 (1870‑1880 mic)

         ·         If only part of the holdings are microfilmed copies, the format used is:

                    EXAMPLE:     Location:     partial runs: LO/N38 A nos 1‑10 (1870‑1880, microfilm: 1876‑1877)

                                          [-- in this case, the word microfilm is written in full]
    23.3.11: Crossreferences to Other Sources 
     ·         Some periodicals have extensive holdings listed in a secondary source, such as in BUCOP or LEIPER.  Rather than transferring the entire list of holdings, it is permissible to crossrefer to the source in question.  These should appear at the end of the string of library holdings listed in this field.  Use upper‑case letters; separate with a semi-colon.

                    EXAMPLE:     Location:   complete runs: LO/N-1 A, LO/N38 A; See BUCOP

    25. Shelfchecked 
    25.1: Definition 
     ·         Researchers who have looked at the primary sources (newspapers, magazines, annual reports etc.) record details of their ‘shelf check’ here.  Note that this field will not be printed as part of the directory.
    25.2: Formatting Rules 

    ·         The following information for every completed shelf‑check is necessary:  library name (using correct abbreviation), date of the shelf‑check, initials of the researcher, and volume and numbers and run dates of issues shelfchecked, and photocopies of title pages taken.

                     FORMAT:        ShelfChecked: [Lib.Code] [Date] [Init.] [Issues checked [Issue mic/photo]]

                     EXAMPLES:   ShelfChecked: LO/N38 A 07 Aug 90 JFK (vol 2 1883, vol 19 1900 [mic vol 2])

                                    ShelfChecked: LO/N-1 A 14 Jul 90 JD (1:1 28 Jul 1871, 14:34 02 Aug 1891 [photocopy 1:1])
    25.3: Shelfchecking Principles 
    25.3.1: General 
     ·         Check the following issues of each journal:

    i.      the first (or earliest available);

    ii.     the earliest in the 1800’s if the title ran pre‑1800;

    iii.    one copy of each change of title, preferably the first after the change;

    iv.    the last copy (//‑‑symbol to indicate discontinuation);

    v.     the last copy in the year 1900 if the title ran post‑1900;

    vi.    the first copy of a new series (to find the reason for the ns numbering).

     ·         Look for new titles fitting the following description:

    i.      published in England

    ii.     at any time between 01 Jan 1800 and 30 Dec 1900;

    iii.    any frequency, daily to annually;

    iv.    any field, including almanacs, government publications;

    v.     any language (see 2.2.6 for more details).

    ·         Watch especially for changes of title and for merges and notices of termination of publication. Give dates and numbering for changes of title and merges.  Note merge dates and titles on main entries of both merging titles.

    ·         Keep as general principles:

    i.      accuracy of detail above mass of detail on any one title;

    ii.     obtain data from most efficient sources first: Colindale Library, British Museum library, university libraries.

     ·         Previously printed catalogues are often in error.  The newspaper itself is the most reliable source, but even it can not always be believed (erratic in numbering: typos, changes from issue to issue).
    25.3.2: Bound Volumes of Miscellaneous Periodicals 
     ·         Sometimes a periodical received may be one of several miscellaneous titles in a bound folio.  It is important, and more efficient, for the researcher to shelfcheck ALL periodicals within the volume, and note accordingly the extra titles shelfchecked.

     ·         NOTE: BRITISH LIBRARY: The British Library has several of these folios, many of which have pressmarks between 1865.? to 1867.? (no P.P.).  Many of these have already been shelfchecked (in summer 1989 mainly); therefore, check a few of the titles to see if they have indeed been done.  In either case, ensure that ALL titles are checked off in the BLC.  If the title is not found in the BLC, record the title on a separate sheet as part of our BLC records.
    25.3.3: Handwritten Shelfchecks 

    ·         There may be occasions where you cannot do a shelfcheck on a computer, such as if there are not enough computers, or one or more are not operating.  Also, sometimes, certain libraries or parts thereof may not allow computers, or there may be a title in another section in the library for which it is more inconvenient to take the computer.  In such instances, you should be prepared to do handwritten shelfchecks.

    ·         There are forms in the office that, if available, may be quicker, but if not, simply use paper from a notebook.  Handwritten shelfchecks should be recorded as legibly as possible, and whenever possible, they should be typed in as soon as can be arranged and by the person who wrote it, in order to avoid confusion or errors in transcription.  Note that this procedure should be used only if time is to be wasted otherwise, since handwritten shelfchecks are more prone to errors.
    26. Comments 
    26.1: Definition 
     ·         This field is used for information that is either not already covered by other fields or that requires clarification.  A combination of direct quotations, both from the periodical itself and secondary sources, along with subjective statements form the bulk of the comments field, which help to give a clear idea and flavour of the periodical.
    26.2: Formatting Rules 
    26.2.1: Introductory Notes 

    ·         Comments are entered as full sentences with capitals and periods.

                     Include:

              1 - MOTTOES

      2 - FOCUS OF PUBLICATION (OFTEN IN PROSPECTUS OR FIRST NUMBER OF PAPER)

    3 - SUBJECTIVE COMMENTS BY THE RESEARCHER

    4 - PUBLICATION IRREGULARITIES

    5 - VARIATIONS IN TITLE OR SUBTITLE (NOT ALREADY COVERED IN THEN TITL/SUBTITL)

    6 - COMMENTS BY OTHER CRITICS, BIBLIOGRAPHERS, ETC. (SECONDARY SOURCES)

     

    ·         Comments by project researchers, critics, bibliographers, or other persons can include such items as:

    1.        significance in politics, religion, circulation

    2.        names of other significant persons and their contribution/importance to the periodical

    3.        significant format information

    4.        titles of important articles

    5.        details on attacks/arguments with rival publications

    6.        internal company politics

    7.        reasons for demise of periodical

     

    ·         In writing comments, strive for clarity and conciseness.  Try and avoid duplication or redundancy.  Also try and avoid excessively long quotes or comments.  A maximum of ONE SCREEN worth of comments (23-25 lines) should serve as a guide for the size of the comments field.

                     NOTE: Do not, however, sacrifice important information in adhering to this maximum.  If in doubt, record the extra comments, and leave a note in the Query field for editing at a later date.

     

    ·         When recording quotes in the comments field, try and avoid unnecessary preparatory phrases if the quote can stand on its own, especially if such a phrase is redundant.

                     EXAMPLES:   Comments:  Stratman says that “this periodical was very short-lived” (Stratman, p.101). 

             [-- it is unnecessary to preface the quote since it is footnoted, therefore put:]

                         Comments:  “This periodical was very short-lived” (Stratman, p.101).

     

          Comments:  The editor states on page 1 of 1:1 that “we are pleased to make your acquaintance” (1:1, p.1).          

                                  [-- again, this is unnecessary - rather, record as:]

                           Comments:  “We are pleased to meet your acquaintance” (1:1, p.1).

                     NOTE: There are, of course, times when adding a preparatory phrase is necessary or makes things smoother.  Here again, keep in mind the desire for the comments to be CLEAR and CONCISE.

                    EXAMPLE:     Comments:    This periodical contains “articles on science, art, literature and music” (1:1, p.2).
    26.2.2: Format for Recording Comments 
     ·         For direct quotes in the comments field use quotation marks (“ ”)

                    EXAMPLE:       Comments: “We profess to be the best paper in all of England.”

    ·         For quotes within quotes, use single quotations marks.  Always input single quotation marks the way they should appear in the final copy.

                    EXAMPLE:     Comments: “This paper shall exemplify the adage -- ‘Art is long, and LIFE but short’.”

    ·         Use the present progressive tense as opposed to past tense when quoting.

                    EXAMPLE:     Comments: The magazine contains...     [-- NOT The magazine contained...]
    26.2.3: Acknowledging Sources 
     ·         Footnote all quotes, whether taken from a primary or secondary source.

                    EXCEPTION: It is not necessary to footnote the motto of a periodical:

                    EXAMPLE:     Comments: Motto: “All for one, and one for all.”

                                                              [Note the format used.]
    26.2.4: Quoting Directly From the Periodical 

    ·         Include the title heading (if available), issue number (or date if no number available) and page number, according to the following format:

    *** NOTE: MEMORIZE THIS FORMAT, AS IT IS IMPORTANT TO BE CONSISTENT ***

            EXAMPLES:   Comments: ... (Introductory Note no 1, p.2).

                             Comments: ...end of quote” (Editor’s Notes 1:6, p.5).

                             Comments: ...enjoy this magazine!” (To our Readers no 1, title page).

                             Comments: ...our first number” (1845, p.1).

                    NOTE: Do not record both the issue number and date (eg. no 1, Jun 1899).  The issue number will suffice, and if absent, then indicate the date.  An exception would be when a periodical has several "no 1's" due to title changes or new series.  In that case, putting both the number and the date would help to clarify things. 

     

    ·         Occasionally a motto or introductory statement is in some sort of poetic form.  Do not try to type this out as formatted in the periodical, ie:

                    EXAMPLE:    

                 Comments:  Motto:  He either fears his faith too much

                                                       Or his deserts are small,

                                                                        That dares not put it to the touch,

                                                                        To gain or lose it all

                [but, rather, type each line continuously, separating each with a slash (/):]

                       Comments:    Motto: “He either fears his faith too much/ Or his deserts are small,/ That dares not put it to the touch,/ To gain or lose it all.”

                    NOTE: A space is placed after the backslash, but not before.
    26.2.5: Quoting Secondary Sources 
     ·         Because all secondary sources quoted in the comments field appear in either the source or history field, an abbreviated form of the bibliographical information suffices, which consists of the author’s name and the page number(s) that the quote was taken from, all within parenthesis.

                    EXAMPLES:   Comments: ... (Jack, p.21).

                                    Comments: ... (Fox-Bourne, p.iii).

     ·         NOTE: If an author published more than one history or source and you quote from both, include the date of publication of the particular secondary source or some other clue to indicate the source in question.

                    EXAMPLE:     Comments: ... (Stratman 1952, p.34). ........  (Stratman 1957, p.2).

    31.1.9: Nicknames 
    ·         If a periodical lists both a person’s real name and his/her nickname, the nickname should be recorded in parentheses alongside the real name.  The nickname should also be added to the NAMES field.

                    EXAMPLES:   Editor :     Mitford, John (‘Mad’ Mitford)
    31.1.10: Royalty 

    ·         Persons with royal titles should be recorded as is.  Actual names, if known, should be indicated and recorded in the Names field.

                    EXAMPLES:   Contributor:   Lord Northcliffe (Alfred Harmsworth)
        Names:   Harmsworth, Alfred

                                          Editor:    Lady Blessington

    ·     If a royal personage was somehow connected to the publication, but it is not clear how, his or her name should be indicated in the names field.

                        EXAMPLES:         Names:  Price Albert

                                            Names:  Queen Victoria
    31.2: Special Cases Within Each Field 

    Issuing bodies and people's names need to have <CCO> added to the end of them if we are going to make a change from lower case to capital.   Unless this is done, the software will not recognize the change.

     

    Example:

    Smith, John P.    would need to be changed to:

    Smith, John P.<CCO>
    31.2.1: Proprietors 

    i.      This information is difficult to obtain.  Sometimes a hand‑written note appears on a publication source.  However, sometimes the name of the distributor of stamps also appears there. So if there is any doubt place a question mark after the name, and a note in the queries field to explain the question mark.

    ii.     In some cases, proprietors died and their trustees would temporarily take over the proprietorship of the publication. The format for this type of entry is:

                    EXAMPLE:     Proprietor:   Smith, John, Trustees of the late (1861)
    31.2.2: Editors 

    i.   Sometimes a periodical may indicate a ‘generic’ or ‘irregular’ editor(s).  Such information should be recorded in the comments field, not in the editors field.

                    EXAMPLES:     Comments:         “Edited by a Society of Gentlemen.”

                                        Comments:         “edited by members of the Garrick Club.”

                                        Comments:         “Edited by a ‘Lady’. ” 

    ii.     ‘Conductor’, ‘manager’, ‘compiler’, ‘supervisor’, ‘literary editor’ and ‘assistant editor’ names should go in editor field and the specific designation of these individuals should be included in parentheses.

                        EXAMPLE:       Editor:        Burroughs, George (compiler 1898 ‑ 1900)

    iii.         As a rule, sub-editors, etc. are listed only if:

    o        few or no other persons connected to the publication are indicated.
    o        the person is a well‑known 19th century figure.

                   EXAMPLE:    Editor:   Dickens, Charles  (sub-editor 1877)
    31.2.3: Publishers 

    i.      Some sources will state, for example, that the periodical is published or printed for some person or company.  Unless this is clarified elsewhere in the periodical, these should be handled in the following manner:

                     “Published by ... for XXX”    --- XXX is the PROPRIETOR

                        “Printed by ... for YYY”    --- YYY is the PUBLISHER

    EXAMPLES:   [Periodical states “Published by Guy Rayner for William Nelson”:]

                                Proprietor:               William Nelson
                                 Publisher:               Guy Rayner

     

                     [Periodical states “Printed by James Tweedie for Abel Heywood & Co”:]

                                Publisher:                Abel Heywood & Co
                                     Printer:                James Tweedie

    {Periodical states: Smithson, John and Co}   - then enter it as follows:  John Smithson & Co.
    31.2.4: Printers 

    1.      Sometimes a periodical is printed in a different place than published.  You can indicate this in brackets in the printer field, or state it in the comments field.

                    EXAMPLES:   Printers:    National Printing Agency (Edinburgh)

                                Comments:    Printed in Leicester.

     

    2.   J. James Jackson  needs to be entered as follows:   Jackson, J. James;  however if it is

    J.James Jackson and Co  - you would leave it as is

     

    3.  .   J. S. Southby & Thomas Green   - would be split up as

    Southby, J.S.

    Green, Thomas
    31.2.5: Contributors 

    i.      Indicate illustrators with (ill.) after their names.

    EXAMPLE:     Contributors:     Dickens, Charles; Cruikshank, George (ill.); Howitt, Mary

    ii.     Other significant information about a contributor and/or their contribution should be included in the comments field.

    EXAMPLE:         Comments:     “George Cruikshank provided all the artwork for this periodical, which consists of 28 full-page colour engravings and numerous other smaller sketches and black and white engravings.”

    iiii.   DO NOT RECORD persons whose work has already been published and is simply reprinted in the periodical.  For instance, Shakespeare should never appear as a contributor even if one of his plays or sonnets appears in a magazine.
    31.3: Names 
    People's names need to have <CCO> added to the end of them if we are going to make a change from lower case to capital.   Unless this is done, the software will not recognize the change.

    Example:

    Kieswetter, john peter   would need to be change to

    Kiewsetter,  John Peter <CCO>
